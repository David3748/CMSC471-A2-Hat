White hat write-up

Our dataset is about maternal mortality which is defined as deaths per 100,000 inhabitants. It has data from around the world for countries and World Bank regional aggregates from 2000 to 2017. It has the income bracket assosciated with each area as well (high, upper middle, middle, lower middle, low). We chose a dumbbell chart to show for each region the change between the start of the data and the end. This allows the viewer to see what has changed the most, as that is affects the length of the line between the 2000 and 2017 ends of the dumbbell. However, since this does not allow the viewer to easily determine whether the maternal mortality rate was increasing or decreasing everywhere without looking at every single data point, we added an short mention of this at the top of the visualization to point out that only the U.S. had an increase in maternal mortality rates. Furthermore, since this is our White hat visualization, we wanted to make it clear in the short text that the U.S. was still much lower than most other places, even if it did get worse since 2000 in 2017. To ensure clarity and accuracy, we wanted to keep the visualization very simple to avoid overwhelming viewers. This meant we colored the data points for 2000 and 2017 different colors and made specific choices of blue, grey, white, black for the entire visualization. We also chose the dumbbell visualization because it allows you to put only 2 data points and a line for all 18 years of data which makes it very simple and intuitive to understand. We also chose to add background lines to make it look a little nicer.

Black hat write-up

The first thing you see in this is the white bold text that says "The US is killing our mothers". This is meant to influence the viewer to start thinking about this even if they don't believe it right away. We then go on to give statistics on how it has risen over 50% from 2000 to 2017 and that the global average went down 38%. We do provide any definition for how maternal mortality rates are calculated and do not mention anything about population increases which could affect it. Furthermore, we have a statistic that it is the #1 fastest increase in any tracked country. We then get to the world map where we brightly colored higher increases and darkly colored decreases so that it is more apparent and draws the eye. There is hover feature which allows the user to see the percent increase/decrease in maternal mortality rates and its place compared to other countries.

There is a second visualization right underneath that one to further drive the point home. We purposefully do include other countries that have large decreases to make the disparity seem even larger. The color coding remains the same as the first graph with the U.S. in a bright orange/red and others in darker shades. We chose red as that is usually associated with bad things. These graphs distort perception by making the viewer think the U.S. maternity rates are really poor when in fact they are some of the best in the world.

During these years, the US changed how it counts maternal deaths

In 2003 the standard US death certificate added a checkbox asking whether a woman who died was pregnant, or had been in the past year. States added it to their own certificates one at a time over the next 14 years.

States whose death certificates had the pregnancy checkbox

Counting the same deaths with the checkbox more than doubled the rate

The CDC's National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) coded the 2016 death records that mentioned a pregnancy twice: once using the checkbox, and once ignoring it, as coders did before 2003.

  • The checkbox catches maternal deaths that doctors didn't connect to pregnancy when writing the cause of death, but it also adds errors. For women aged 40–54, it multiplied maternal deaths fourteenfold (523 instead of 37 over 2015–2016). Without the checkbox, about half of those deaths would have been coded to cancer or heart disease.
  • Because states weren't counting the same way, NCHS stopped publishing an official national maternal mortality rate after 2007.
  • The World Bank numbers in the charts above are modeled estimates rather than NCHS counts, but for the US they draw on these same death records. So part of the US rise from 12 to 19 likely reflects how deaths were counted rather than added risk. NCHS says its study can't tell how much of the extra count is better detection and how much is error.

Source: Hoyert, Uddin and Miniño, Evaluation of the Pregnancy Status Checkbox on the Identification of Maternal Deaths, NCHS, 2020: Table I for when each state added the checkbox, Table B for the rates. States count once their own question had the same detail as the standard checkbox; mid-year additions count in that year. California kept a simpler yes/no question. The 2016 rates cover all 50 states and DC; the with-checkbox rate counts California and West Virginia, which lacked the checkbox, the old way. NCHS rates differ from the World Bank's modeled estimates.
